\small
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
\item This launch supplies the eleven-byte slice \texttt{-2147483648}. The function has returned at the same observation marker. Its status describes failure under this exercise\textquotesingle s checked-literal contract, even though the written mathematical integer is the signed-word minimum.
\item \texttt{\$v0 = -2} reports overflow. \texttt{\$v1 = 10} identifies the final digit, not the unary sign at offset 0. Each decimal literal must stay within 0 through 2147483647 while its digits are accumulated. Including the last \texttt{8} would make the positive magnitude 2147483648, so the literal fails before unary minus is applied.
\item After the four-byte guard, the input bytes are \texttt{2d 32 31 34 37 34 38 33 36 34 38}. The sign is \texttt{2d}; the detecting digit is the last \texttt{38}, at \texttt{0x1001000e}. Positions count bytes from the supplied pointer, not printed digits alone. The next byte is mapped padding and lies beyond length 11.
\item The row at \texttt{0x100100d0} contains length, caller status, caller second result, and the final guard. Bytes \texttt{63 00 00 00} encode decimal 99, the initial value of both result words. The evaluator returns through registers and leaves caller data read-only. Continuing past \texttt{expression\_continue} lets the caller replace those two words; exit later reuses \texttt{\$v0} for its service number. Length is \texttt{0b 00 00 00}, or 11. A successful route to the minimum value is \texttt{-2147483647-1}: its literal and unary step fit, then the checked subtraction reaches $-2147483648$. That is a different input with a different sequence of checks.
\end{enumerate}
